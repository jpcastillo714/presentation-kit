Part 2 · The limit

A constant offset survives any amount of averaging

If every reading is shifted by the same amount b (a ruler that starts at 0.3 mm, a clock that runs fast), the average converges to the truth plus b. The total error falls as σ/√N until it meets |b|, and stays there.

Your ruler reads 0.3 mm too long, and σ = 1 mm. After averaging 10 000 readings, the error of the average is about…

0.3 mm. The random part fell to σ/√10 000 = 0.01 mm, but the offset is in every reading, so it is in the average too. More data cannot reveal it; only a comparison with something known can (a calibration).

What to do with a floor

  • Find it before you collect more data: past the floor, extra hours of measurement buy nothing.
  • Measure the offset against a reference (a calibration standard, a blank, a known source), then subtract it.
  • Report both parts: the random error, which shrinks with N, and the systematic one, which does not.
Check yourself: you averaged 1 million readings and quote an error of σ/1000. What is missing?

The systematic error. σ/√N only describes the random part; an offset of any size would leave that number unchanged.
