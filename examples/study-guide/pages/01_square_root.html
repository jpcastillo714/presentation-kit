Part 1 · The rule

Averaging N measurements divides the scatter by √N, not by N

Each measurement is the true value plus noise. Noise of opposite signs partly cancels in a sum, so the average scatters around the truth by σ/√N, where σ is the scatter of one measurement.

Imagine measuring the same length many times with a ruler whose readings scatter by σ = 1 mm. One reading can be off by about a millimetre. What happens when you average several?

You average 4 times as many readings. The scatter of the average…

A half: √4 = 2. The noise does not vanish in proportion to the data, because the cancellation is only partial. The simulator below repeats the experiment many times so you can see it.

scatter of the average: mm σ/√N = mm

Read the rule on a log–log plot

On logarithmic axes, σ/√N is a straight line with slope −½: every factor of 100 in N buys a factor of 10 in precision. That is why the last digit of a measurement is so expensive.

Check yourself: you need an error 3 times smaller. How many more readings?

9 times more: the error goes as 1/√N, so N must grow by 3² = 9.

Check yourself: why is it √N and not N?

The sum of N independent noises has a variance N·σ² (variances add), so its scatter is √N·σ. Dividing the sum by N to get the average leaves σ·√N / N = σ/√N.
